# Style DNA

Load this when style fidelity matters, when writing prompts, or when an output
feels like a generic infographic.

## One sentence

Pure white space, thin wobbly black line work, and deadpan Inkblot doing one
strange useful job; add machinery and notes only when the idea needs them.

## Must-have traits

- Standalone 16:9 horizontal illustration.
- Pure white background: no beige, warm gray, paper texture, gradients,
  shadows, noise, or complex scenery.
- Thin, slightly wobbly black hand-drawn line art; never vector-perfect or
  thick marker cartoon.
- Inkblot is the main or only visible subject unless the user asks to explain a
  real system.
- One image explains one question, action, state, structure, or metaphor.
- Inkblot operates the mechanism; removing Inkblot should break the scene.

## Mode defaults

**Inkblot-first:** use at least 50% blank space, one Inkblot, 0-2 essential
props, and no labels by default. Add at most three 1-3 word labels only when
they materially improve the idea. Inkblot must remain the visual focal point;
make every prop smaller and subordinate. Never use an oversized prop. Do not
add background machinery.

**System portrait:** use at least 35% blank space, one physical mechanism,
roughly 3-6 verified elements, and up to five short labels only when helpful. Keep Inkblot
causal rather than decorative.

## System portrait character

System portraits look like a cutaway workshop, repair manual doodle, backstage
scene, or strange physical machine. They do not look like an architecture
diagram that happened to receive hand-drawn boxes.

Use physical continuity to show relationships: one pipe connects two real
objects, one parcel changes hands, one cable reaches a switch, or one drawer
holds state. Preserve direction and avoid decorative arrows.

Keep exact title, scope, technology, legend, and relationship prose in the
caption or truth ledger. The image canvas stays quiet.

## Color rules

- **Black:** line art, Inkblot, key objects, most labels.
- **Orange:** the one primary flow, route, handoff, or movement.
- **Red:** failure, friction, risk, alarm, or important result.
- **Blue:** state, feedback, secondary context, AI/assistant signal, or an
  intentionally unresolved edge.

Use less color than feels necessary. Never rely on color alone; the object,
line, gesture, or label must carry the same meaning.

## US English localization

Use American English spelling and short whiteboard-like fragments: `front
door`, `quiet queue`, `state shelf`, `health bell`, `manual handoff`, `trust
edge`, `retry`, `safe copy`.

Everyday objects may ground the scene: cardboard boxes, receipts, mailboxes,
garage pegboards, diner counters, laundromat carts, school lockers, old utility
rooms, workbenches, and ticket windows. Use only what clarifies the mechanism.

"American" means US English and everyday editorial grounding, not flags,
eagles, monuments, cowboy imagery, patriotic palettes, or stereotypes.

## Avoid

- Mermaid, UML, network, or C4 boxes-and-arrows rendered in a sketch font.
- PPT infographic, course slide, dashboard, realistic UI, or cloud-icon wall.
- Dense labels, numbered instructions, code, ports, or full protocol prose in
  the image.
- Children's cartoon, mascot sticker, kawaii expression, meme face, or logo.
- Polished commercial illustration, glossy 3D, background scenery, or visual
  clutter.
- A top-left title such as `System Architecture`, `Workflow`, or `Roadmap`.
